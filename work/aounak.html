AOUNAK: booking a truck to move furniture, in five steps

Role Co-founder, UX/UI designerPlatform iOS and Android, Arabic and EnglishStatus Designed and tested, never launched
The short version

A family member bought a barbecue grill and couldn't get it home the same day. That became a startup idea: an app to book a truck for moving furniture, as easily as booking a ride. I ran the whole UX process myself, from interviews and surveys to a competitor audit, card sorting, usability testing and an A/B test. The result was a booking flow that takes five steps. We never launched, and I'll explain why at the end.

Two sides, both losing

Customers spent ages finding someone with a truck, got a different price from every driver, and had no guarantee their furniture would arrive in one piece. Meanwhile, drivers waited on the street for hours hoping for a few clients.

Our product vision was simple: anyone can book a safe, fairly priced pickup in four or five steps at most.

What people told me

I interviewed 6 people one-to-one and surveyed 15 more, then grouped everything into an affinity map. Some of what they said:

"Prices aren't consistent. There's no clear formula."
"It takes too much time to find someone."
"My furniture got damaged a little."
"Freelance drivers don't meet deadlines."
"I can't choose the size of the pickup."
"I can only pay in cash."
"I like being able to track the driver on the map."
"I can move right away instead of waiting 72 hours."
From my affinity map. Lightly edited for grammar.

Where the competition fell short

I profiled three competitors and ran a heuristic evaluation on the strongest one, MoveAdvisor. It scored 74% overall, but look at where it lost points:

Search100%
Trust and credibility88%
Layout and visuals83%
Forms74%
Task flow65%
Navigation57%
Help and errors54%

Navigation, task flow and help were the weak spots. A short, guided booking flow could win on exactly those.

Three people we designed for

  • Yasser just bought a barbecue grill and wants it home today.
  • Muhannad needs his damaged couch taken to a carpenter, at a fair price.
  • Saeed runs a delivery fleet that's short on trucks. He needs two or three medium pickups, urgently.

I also built two personas, a journey map from first search to feedback, and the app's structure through card sorting and tree testing.

What testing changed

Three people tried two tasks on the wireframes: register, then request a pickup. Both tasks were easy to finish, but getting around the app was a little hard. So:

  • I split booking into one step per screen, with a clear Next button.
  • I added driver ratings and trip counts, because people wanted to know who was coming.
  • Testers also asked for a sign-up confirmation, social sign-in and adding their address at registration. Those were planned for the next round.

Then I ran an A/B test on the pickup details screen.

The vision said four or five steps. Booking takes five.

1. Truck sizeLarge, medium or small
2. PickupCurrent location or search
3. Drop-offRoute on the map
4. WhenNow or scheduled
5. ConfirmPrice, distance, driver rating
Then trackA live countdown to arrival

For whole-home moves, I added a second service: a short quote form for a moving crew, asking about rooms, furniture, floor, elevator and assembly.

Why it never launched

The product was ready to build. Then my co-founders moved abroad, one to Canada and one to Qatar. I didn't want to run it alone, so we closed the company. There are no live numbers to show.

If it had launched, I'd have tracked how many people finished all five steps, how long it took from opening the app to a confirmed pickup, and how many drivers signed up and came back.

What I took from it

Research changed this design more than any visual polish did. The first version was plain, with no clear journey. Talking to people and watching them use it gave it one.

It's also why I push for even small, quick rounds of research on client projects that say they don't have time for it.
