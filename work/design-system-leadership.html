Giving a gov-tech company's products one fingerprint

Role UI/UX leadTeam 4 designersClient a Saudi government-tech company, through Synechron
The short version

For one Saudi government-tech client I led two projects. First, the design of four products for a Saudi transport authority. Second, the client's own design system: its products' components were fragmented, with no unified experience between them. I led four designers to build one hybrid design system on top of the Saudi national design system: 8 foundations, 30+ components, light and dark tokens, Arabic RTL and a guidebook. Halfway through, the project stopped for seven months. Getting it moving again taught me the most.

Two projects, one client

The products. I led the design team on four products for a Saudi transport authority, from user stories to final UI.

The design system. Separately, the client's own products had no shared components. Buttons, inputs, tables and status labels were built differently in each one, so there was no unified experience between them. The design system was there to fix that.

Why we went hybrid

We had three options, and the decision shaped everything after it.

OptionGoodBad
Use the national design system (DGA) as it isFast, and aligned with the standardNo identity for the client, and gaps for the dense tables and dashboards their products needed
Build everything from scratchFull controlSlow, and it drifts away from the national standard
✓ A hybridThe national system as the base, the client's brand and missing patterns on topNeeds clear rules about what can change, so we wrote them

We also didn't start from zero. A designer before us had begun a library, so we kept what worked, and used Google's Material Design for the patterns the national system didn't cover.

All of it sits on light and dark colour tokens, an 8-pixel spacing grid, and Arabic RTL built into the system rather than added later.

A design system is mostly habits

Components are the visible part. What keeps a system alive is how the team works on it, so I spent as much time on that as on the library.

  1. A 10 a.m. stand-up every day to review changes and updates together.
  2. A shared work log where every designer wrote down what they'd built and why.
  3. A critique before anything was merged: each designer presented their work and got feedback first.
  4. Rules for anyone joining: what can change, how to name things, who approves.

I also wrote the guidebook: one document for new designers, BAs, developers and management, covering tokens, components, version control, governance, onboarding and how to contribute.

Then the project stopped for seven months.

When it came back, the momentum and most of the context were gone. It would have been easy to pick up the components where we'd left off. I rebuilt the plan first instead: a design system plan, an onboarding plan, an audit sheet, a tracking sheet and a presentation for the directors, so everyone agreed again on what we were doing and why.

A new designer joined and was contributing within weeks, because the onboarding was now written down instead of living in my head.

How we checked it

We tested the design system on real work: we applied it to parts of the transport authority's products, using the library's components and variants in place of one-off parts. It proved very efficient.

Before handing anything to developers, we also reviewed every component for consistent naming, complete states and variants, and the final colour variables, and ran a separate review of whether each component was ready to build.

The programme didn't budget for usability testing with users. It's the first thing I'd add next time.

What I'd do differently

  • Measure adoption from day one. How many screens use the library tells you more than how many components exist.
  • Keep the directors close. A short monthly update might have prevented the pause.
  • Bring developers in earlier, so readiness isn't the last step.
